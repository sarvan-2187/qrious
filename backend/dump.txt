ID: bell-state
--- CIRCUIT ---
```text
q0: |0⟩ ──H────■──── M
               │
q1: |0⟩ ───────X──── M
```
The H (Hadamard) gate creates the superposition on the top qubit. The vertical line represents the CNOT gate. It entangles the two wires.
--- MATH ---
Start with the initial state: 
$$|\psi \rangle = |00\rangle$$

Apply the Hadamard gate to the first qubit: 
$$|\psi \rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \otimes |0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |10\rangle)$$

Apply the CNOT gate (flips the second qubit if the first is 1): 
$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$$

When measured in the computational basis, the probability of $|00\rangle$ is $\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = 0.5$, and the probability of $|11\rangle$ is $0.5$.
--- TRY IT ---
Experiment 1: Run the default circuit and observe the histogram. Notice the 50/50 split between 00 and 11.
Experiment 2: Change the initial state of q 0 from ∣0⟩ to ∣1⟩ (apply an X gate before the H gate). Run the circuit again. You have created a different Bell state: ∣Φ−⟩.
Experiment 3: Apply an X gate to q 1 before the entangling step. Notice how the histogram now perfectly correlates 01 and 10 (∣Ψ+⟩).

--- CIRCUIT ---

State 1: |ψ⟩ ───────X───────── │
State 2: |φ⟩ ───────X───────── The ancilla is split into ∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩. If the ancilla is ∣0⟩, nothing happens (the states remain ∣ψ⟩∣φ⟩). If the ancilla is ∣1⟩, the states are swapped (∣φ⟩∣ψ⟩). --- MATH ---

ID: no-cloning-theorem-demonstration
--- CIRCUIT ---
There is no circuit for this, because the circuit is physically impossible! A common beginner mistake is thinking a CNOT gate copies states. q0: |ψ⟩ ──■── (Control) │ q1: |0⟩ ──X── (Target) If ∣ψ⟩=∣1⟩, the CNOT produces ∣11⟩ (looks like a copy). If ∣ψ⟩=∣0⟩, the CNOT produces ∣00⟩ (looks like a copy). But if ∣ψ⟩= ∣0⟩+∣1⟩ , the 2 CNOT produces ∣00⟩+∣11⟩ (a Bell state), which is not two independent copies of ∣ψ⟩. 2
--- MATH ---
Let U be our hypothetical cloning operator. Assume it clones state ∣ψ⟩: $$ U(|\psi\rangle|0\rangle) = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle $$ Assume it also clones state ∣φ⟩: $$ U(|\phi\rangle|0\rangle) = |\phi\rangle|\phi\rangle $$ Take the inner product of the two initial states: $$ (\langle\psi|\langle 0|) \cdot (|\phi\rangle|0\rangle) = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle \langle 0|0\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle $$ Take the inner product of the two final (cloned) states: $$ (\langle\psi|\langle\psi|) \cdot (|\phi\rangle|\phi\rangle) = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = (\langle\psi|\phi\rangle)^2 $$ Because U is a unitary operator, the inner product before must equal the inner product after: $$ x = x^2 $$ where x=⟨ψ∣φ⟩. The equation x=x2 has only two solutions: x=0 or x=1. If x=1, the states are identical (∣ψ⟩=∣φ⟩). If x=0, the states are perfectly orthogonal (like ∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩). Conclusion: You can only build a cloning machine that copies classical, orthogonal basis states (like 0 and 1). You cannot build a cloning machine for an arbitrary, unknown quantum superposition.
--- TRY IT ---
Experiment 1 (The CNOT Illusion): Prepare q 0 in the ∣0⟩ state. Apply CNOT with target q 1. Result: 00. (Looks like a clone).
Experiment 2 (The CNOT Illusion): Prepare q 0 in the ∣1⟩ state. Apply CNOT with target q 1. Result: 11. (Looks like a clone).
Experiment 3 (The Proof): Apply a Hadamard to q 0 creating superposition. Apply the CNOT. You have now created a Bell state. The two qubits are entangled, but they are NOT independent clones of the original superposition. This interactive circuit proves that the "copying" breaks down the moment superposition is introduced. --- CIRCUIT ---
Reg 1 (x): |0⟩ ──H^{\otimes n}────| |────H^{\otimes n}──── M | Uf |
Reg 2 (y): |0⟩ ───────────────────| |───────────────────── Unlike previous algorithms, we need a full n-qubit second register to store the output of the function, because the function is not simply a 1-bit boolean output; it outputs n-bit strings.
--- MATH ---
After the oracle, the state is entangled: $$ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in {0,1}^n} |x\rangle |f(x)\rangle $$ Because f(x) is 2-to-1, for every output z, there are two inputs x and x⊕s.
